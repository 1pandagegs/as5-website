The Case for Building Slower

Speed sells. Sequence delivers. The projects that last are rarely the ones that moved fastest through their first year.

Speed is the easiest thing to sell in development. A faster timeline sounds like efficiency, like competence, like less risk. Often, it's the opposite.

The projects that get built fastest are frequently the ones where planning was compressed to protect a schedule — where questions that should have taken weeks to resolve were answered in days, and the answers were locked in before anyone could fully stress-test them.

None of this shows up immediately. It shows up two, five, ten years later: in maintenance costs that should have been designed out, in layouts that don't quite work the way anyone intended, in the quiet sense that a building never became what it was supposed to be.

This isn't an argument for slowness as a virtue in itself. It's an argument for sequence: plan thoroughly, then move with discipline — rather than moving quickly and hoping the plan holds up.

The developments that are still performing well decades after handover were rarely the fastest ones to break ground. They were the ones where speed was earned by getting the sequence right, not substituted for it.
